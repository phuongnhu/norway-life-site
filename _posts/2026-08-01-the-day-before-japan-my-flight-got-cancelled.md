The message came in the early morning, one day before I was supposed to fly to Japan. Finnair had cancelled my flight. The reason given was "technical." I read it maybe four times, as if reading it again would somehow change the words.

Japan had been on my mind for a long time. When I first started thinking about a summer trip, it was the very first place that popped into my head. Then I talked myself toward somewhere nearer, looked at a lot of options, and in the end came back to Japan anyway and booked it in June. So the booking itself was fairly last minute, but the wish was not new at all. Nine nights, solo, my first time there. The hotels were booked, the ryokan was booked, the suitcase was almost packed. And now, one text message, and it all felt like it was falling apart.

I want to tell you the whole story, because it ends well, and because I did one thing right and one thing that was almost very wrong.

### My first reaction: cancel everything

Here is the honest part. My first thought was not "let me find another flight." My first thought was "that is it, I will cancel the whole trip."

Part of it was the word "technical." A "technical reason" sounds like the plane is broken, like something is unsafe, and that is not what you want to be thinking about the day before you fly across the world. And then the other thoughts came in fast: maybe I would even save some money if I just cancelled, and did I really want to drag myself that far anyway? Travelling to the other side of the planet suddenly sounded exhausting. In that moment I genuinely felt like I did not want to go anymore, and I sat there ready to write the whole thing off.

I am so glad I did not touch anything for the first hour. Because that feeling was not real. It was exhaustion talking, and I will come back to that at the end, because it turned out to be the most useful thing I learned that day.

### What actually happens when they cancel

Once I calmed down enough to actually read past the word "cancelled," the situation was much smaller than my panic.

When you fly from an airport in the EU or EEA (and Norway counts), the airline cannot just cancel your flight and wish you good luck. They have to get you to your destination, and they have to pay for it. That is your right, not a favour. So Finnair had already done the work: they rebooked me, for free, onto Air France from Oslo to Paris, and then ANA from Paris to Tokyo. Same destination airport, Haneda, only about two hours later than my original plan. I did not pay one krone extra.

You might be wondering about compensation, since you sometimes hear about getting money back for a cancelled flight. In my case there was none, and that is fair enough. Those cash payouts are mainly for when the airline gets you there many hours late, and I landed only about two hours behind my original plan. So I did not receive any money, and honestly I did not even try to claim. Getting to Japan close to on time and at no extra cost was all I actually needed.

One thing I want to underline for anyone reading this before their own trip: do not cancel your hotels the second your flight falls apart. My accommodation was mostly non-refundable, and if I had panic-cancelled those in the first hour, I would have lost real money for nothing. The flight got fixed. The trip was intact the whole time. I just could not see it through the stress.

### A small tip that saved me a lot of worry

There was one more moment of stress, and it taught me something practical that I want to pass on.

Finnair had rebooked me, but my new flights were operated by other airlines, so the Finnair app did not show me much about the new journey. For a moment that emptiness made me nervous, like nothing was really confirmed.

The fix was simple once I understood it. I looked up my new flight number in the Air France app instead, the airline actually operating the first leg, and there it all was: check-in, seat, baggage, everything, exactly as it should be. So if you ever get rebooked onto a different airline, do not panic when your original airline's app looks half-empty. Find the flight in the operating airline's app, and the real information is usually sitting right there.

### The accidental upgrade

And here is the small gift hiding inside the whole mess: ANA is a five-star airline, genuinely nicer than the flight I had originally booked. So the cancellation actually upgraded my long trip across the world. I joked to myself that I hoped they would cancel my return flight too and put me on ANA again (they did not, haha, but a person can dream).

There was another nice surprise waiting in Paris. My memory of that airport, from a trip earlier this year, was genuinely bad: being bussed out across the tarmac to board the plane (which I hate), and then a long, crowded bus ride between terminals when I transited, the whole thing slow and inefficient. So I had been dreading the transfer. This time was the opposite. I took the driverless train between the terminals, quick and simple, and Terminal 1 was actually beautiful and comfortable. The airport I remembered as chaos had somehow turned into a pleasant place to wait, and the long layover I had feared became a calm buffer instead of a rush.

And the thing I was most nervous about, my suitcase making it across two different airlines, it arrived in Tokyo exactly as it should.

### The thing I actually want you to remember

So the practical lesson is easy: know that the airline has to rebook you for free, do not cancel your hotels in a panic, and if you get moved onto a different airline, check that airline's own app for the real details.

But the real lesson was about me, not the flight. That first hour, I was completely sure I did not want to go anymore. I believed it. And it was simply not true. It was stress wearing the costume of "I have changed my mind." Once the logistics were solved, the excitement came straight back, like it had never left, because it never had.

I think this happens to a lot of us, and not only with travel. A hard moment hits, and the tiredness whispers that you want to quit the thing, when really you just want the hard moment to be over. Those two feelings can be almost impossible to tell apart when you are in them. The only trick I have found is time: do not make the big decision in the first hour. Let the panic drain out, and then see what you actually want. Almost always, it is different from what the stress was telling you.

I flew the next day. Japan was everything I hoped, and more, and to think I nearly cancelled it from my own sofa over a text message.
